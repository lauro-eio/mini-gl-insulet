# Case 1 — Marisol and the Onboarding Gap

## Opening

Marisol supervises a six-person assembly cell at a small electronics plant. Over the past year, her cell built a quiet reputation: "the station that catches its own mistakes." No one taught this formally—it grew out of a habit Marisol started after a costly rework incident: every operator does a 30-second self-check before passing a unit down the line, and says out loud what they checked. New teammates picked it up by watching, not by reading a manual.

## Context

This month, three experienced operators transferred to a new shift, and three new hires backfilled the cell in the same week. The self-check ritual has already slipped twice—once dropped entirely during a rush order, once done silently instead of out loud, which meant no one else could catch a miss. Marisol's manager only tracks first-pass yield and does not know the ritual exists as a named practice; he just sees the number was strong last quarter and assumes it will hold.

Marisol notices the new hires treat the self-check as an optional courtesy rather than "how we work here." One new hire, told to hurry during a rush order, skipped it and said, "nobody told me it was mandatory." He was technically right—it was never written down.

## Decision point

Yield numbers still look fine this week, but Marisol can feel the ritual thinning. She can:

1. Write the self-check into the formal SOP and treat it as a compliance item to enforce.
2. Pair each new hire with a senior operator for two weeks specifically to narrate and model the identity behind the check, not just the steps.
3. Wait and see—yield is still acceptable, and she does not want to seem like she is micromanaging a team that used to run itself.

## Discussion questions

1. What is the difference between teaching the self-check as a rule versus transferring it as an identity—and why does that difference matter when experienced people rotate out?
2. How should Marisol respond to the new hire who skipped the check "because no one told him it was mandatory"? What does her answer teach the rest of the cell?
3. Design one visible, repeatable moment this week where the cell can say out loud "this is who we are," even under a rush order.
