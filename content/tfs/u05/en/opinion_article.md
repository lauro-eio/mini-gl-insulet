# The Overtime Announcement I Rewrote Before I Hit Send

I had the message half-typed before I'd thought it through: "If we don't cover this shift, we lose the whole week's incentive." True, technically. It would also have worked—my crew hates losing something they already feel is theirs more than they like earning something new. That's exactly why I stopped typing.

Two nights earlier, a different call had gone sideways because of the same instinct. A door sensor on dock 3 had tripped twice in an hour, an unusual pattern for that door. I told the crew lead, "if we hold the truck to check it, we lose the cutoff for sure." Loss language, said fast, under pressure. He overrode the sensor and moved the truck. Nothing went wrong that night. But when I replayed it, I realized I had framed a safety check as a guaranteed loss and an override as the only way to avoid it—when the honest frame was closer to "a small, checkable risk against a cutoff we could probably still make with four extra minutes." I had picked the frame that got the fastest yes, not the one that matched the facts.

So before sending the overtime message, I made myself do one thing: say the same facts the other way. Instead of "you'll lose the incentive," I wrote "everyone keeps the incentive if we cover this together." Same shift, same pay, same deadline. But now I noticed something—stated as a gain, the ask felt smaller to me, less urgent, more like something I'd actually explain rather than push. That gap told me the loss version had been doing work the facts alone didn't justify.

I sent the gain-framed version. I also went back to the dock 3 override from two nights before and did the reframe test on that one, late, after the fact: "hold four minutes to check a sensor with an unusual pattern" versus "guarantee we lose the cutoff." Once I said it the second way, it was obvious the first frame had been the honest one, and fear of the deadline had picked the second frame for me in the moment.

Now, before I announce any call that involves risk, loss, or overtime, I ask myself which frame I reached for first and whether I would still choose it if I stated the same facts the opposite way. I am not trying to slow every announcement down or hedge every message into mush—the crew needs clear, fast direction most shifts. I am trying to catch the handful of calls where the wording, not the situation, was about to make the decision for me.
