# Case 2 — "We Should Have Seen It Coming": Hindsight in the Incident Review

## Opening

Tomás leads a shift on a packaging line. A near-miss last week — a guard panel left unlatched for about ten minutes before someone noticed — triggered a formal incident review. The review panel includes his ops manager and a safety analyst, neither of whom was on shift that night.

## Context

The review's early conclusion, stated in the first five minutes: "the warning signs were there — this should have been caught sooner." The panel points to a maintenance note from three days earlier that mentioned the same panel's latch "feeling loose."

Tomás pulls the shift log for the ten minutes in question. It shows two routine safety walks were completed in that window, both logged as clear, and the maintenance note in question was one of forty-one similar minor notes filed that same week across the line, none flagged as urgent by the maintenance lead who reviewed them.

| Signal | Known before the incident? | How it looked at the time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Maintenance note on panel latch | Yes, 3 days prior | 1 of 41 minor notes that week; not flagged urgent |
| Safety walk during the 10-minute window | Yes, logged twice | Both walks logged the panel as latched and clear |
| Guard panel actually unlatched | No — only visible after the fact | Not visible on either walk's checklist item |

Once the panel is told the outcome, the maintenance note reads like an obvious red flag. Before the outcome, it was one ordinary note among dozens, and two separate checks during the exact window in question came back clear.

## Decision point

Tomás can:

1. Accept the panel's "should have been caught sooner" framing to close the review quickly and avoid conflict with his ops manager.
2. Push back only verbally, without documentation, and hope the point lands without escalating tension.
3. Present the safety-walk logs and the note-volume data before the review concludes, explicitly separating what was knowable during the window from what is obvious now that the outcome is known.

## Discussion questions

1. What turned an ordinary maintenance note into a "warning sign" only after the incident, and would the panel have flagged it the same way beforehand?
2. How should a review credit or fault a decision based on what was knowable at the time, versus what the outcome later revealed?
3. Design a review-opening step that would keep a panel from reaching a hindsight-driven conclusion before checking the contemporaneous record.
