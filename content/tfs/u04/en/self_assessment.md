# Self-Assessment — Are You Debriefing the Shift or Editing Its Story?

Answer for how you **actually** lead this month (A=1 … D=4). Be honest—this is diagnostic, not a performance review.

--- SECTION 1: QUESTIONS ---

**Q1.** When your team tells the story of how an issue got caught, you usually:
- A) Accept the story as told—it usually matches what happened
- B) Accept it, then occasionally check the log if something seems off
- C) Check the timestamps or log before the story becomes the official version
- D) Require the log to be read before anyone tells the story, every time

**Q2.** After an incident, when someone says "we should have seen that coming," you:
- A) Agree—in hindsight, it does look obvious
- B) Sometimes push back, sometimes go along with it
- C) Ask what was actually visible or known at the time it happened
- D) Treat "we should have seen it" as a claim that needs evidence, not just a mood, every time

**Q3.** When judging whether a call your team made was a good one, you weigh:
- A) Mostly how it turned out—good result, good call
- B) Mostly the result, but you note it if the process seemed weak
- C) The reasoning available at the time, regardless of how it turned out
- D) A standing rule to separate decision quality from result in every review

**Q4.** When rating how a shift went overall, your rating is most shaped by:
- A) However it ended—that is what sticks
- B) The ending, mostly, unless something else stood out
- C) You deliberately look across the whole shift, not just the end
- D) You use a log-based measure that spans the whole shift, not memory of the end

**Q5.** When two team members remember an event differently, you:
- A) Go with whoever tells it more confidently or clearly
- B) Go with the confident version unless someone objects
- C) Check the log or timestamps to settle it, not confidence
- D) Always default to the recorded evidence over any verbal account, no exceptions

**Q6.** A quieter or newer team member's account of who caught a problem first:
- A) Rarely comes up—the loudest account usually stands
- B) Sometimes gets added after the main story is already set
- C) You actively ask for it before the story is finalized
- D) It has a guaranteed slot in the debrief structure, every time

**Q7.** When your own memory of a past decision feels very clear and certain, you:
- A) Trust it fully—clear memory means accurate memory
- B) Mostly trust it, unless there is an obvious contradiction
- C) Recognize that certainty and accuracy are not the same thing, and check when it matters
- D) Treat high personal certainty about a past call as a specific prompt to verify against the record

**Q8.** Your team's culture around debriefs and incident reviews is:
- A) Whoever tells the clearest story sets the record
- B) Informal—stories and facts mix with no clear order
- C) Facts get established before the narrative, most of the time
- D) There is a visible, protected ritual: log first, story second, every time, even under pressure to wrap up fast

--- SECTION 2: SCORING ---

Score A=1, B=2, C=3, D=4. Total range 8–32.

--- SECTION 3: PROFILES ---

1. **Story-First Narrator (8–14):** The clearest voice in the room usually becomes the official record, and hindsight quietly fills in the rest. Useful for morale and quick closure; risky when the goal is learning what actually happened. Pick one recurring debrief and require the timestamp log before anyone tells the story.
2. **Occasional Fact-Checker (15–20):** You check the record sometimes, usually once something already feels off. Build one standing rule: read the log before the story, not after a doubt appears.
3. **Evidence-Led Reviewer (21–26):** You already separate what was knowable at the time from what looks obvious now, most of the time. Strengthen it by naming the practice out loud so quieter voices know their account has a guaranteed place.
4. **Ritual Guardian (27–32):** You protect a visible, log-first debrief ritual even under pressure to wrap up quickly. Teach one direct report to run it so the ritual survives when you are off-shift.
