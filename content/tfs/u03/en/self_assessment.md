# Self-Assessment — Are You Reasoning With Numbers or Reacting to Them?

Answer for how you **actually** lead this month (A=1 … D=4). Be honest—this is diagnostic, not a performance review.

--- SECTION 1: QUESTIONS ---

**Q1.** When a shift's defect count spikes well above normal, you:
- A) Escalate immediately based on the raw number
- B) Mention it and watch informally, without checking further
- C) Check how many units the count is based on before deciding how to react
- D) Compare it against the trailing baseline and required sample size before acting, every time

**Q2.** When one operator or line posts an unusually great result, you:
- A) Assume it reflects a stable new skill and plan to roll it out immediately
- B) Praise it without examining why it happened
- C) Note it could be partly chance and wait for another data point before concluding
- D) Explicitly expect some pull-back next period and say so to the team in advance

**Q3.** When a number arrives packaged with a plausible cause ("the new hire caused the spike"), you:
- A) Accept the causal story because it matches what you already expected
- B) Mostly accept it, with a passing thought that it could be coincidence
- C) Check how often a swing this size happens anyway before accepting the story
- D) Always check the base rate first and only accept the causal story if it adds something the base rate cannot explain

**Q4.** Comparing your gut call on a decision you make often (pull a batch, approve rework, escalate a count) to a simple written rule, you:
- A) Trust your gut—rules just slow you down
- B) Use the rule sometimes, when it is convenient
- C) Default to the rule for repeat decisions and rarely override it
- D) Follow the rule and log any override with a specific reason, every time

**Q5.** Before treating a small sample of defects as a real signal, you:
- A) Do not think about the sample size at all
- B) Think about it briefly, then decide on gut anyway
- C) Ask how many units the count is based on
- D) Estimate whether a sample that size could produce this result by chance alone

**Q6.** When a record-good week is followed by a normal or worse week, you:
- A) Look for someone or something to blame for the drop
- B) Feel confused about the drop but move on without examining it
- C) Recognize this could simply be the number returning to normal
- D) Had already told the team beforehand that a pull-back was likely, not a failure

**Q7.** Your habit of checking the numbers before reacting to an unusual result is:
- A) Nonexistent—you react to the headline number as given
- B) Informal and inconsistent
- C) A personal habit you follow most of the time
- D) A named, visible team routine that survives deadline pressure

**Q8.** Under pressure to give a fast verdict on a numbers-based call, you:
- A) Give the verdict the raw number suggests
- B) Give the fast verdict, then quietly worry about it afterward
- C) Protect a short check on calls that will repeat or drive a bigger decision
- D) Follow a pre-agreed rule for which calls always get the short check, regardless of pressure

--- SECTION 2: SCORING ---

Score A=1, B=2, C=3, D=4. Total range 8–32.

--- SECTION 3: PROFILES ---

1. **Headline Reactor (8–14):** You act on the number as presented, without checking sample size or expecting a pull-back. Fast, but exposed on calls where a small count or one outlier is doing all the talking. Pick one recurring numbers-based call and add a ten-second sample-size question before you act on it.
2. **Occasional Skeptic (15–20):** You sometimes question a striking number, usually after a swing has already caused confusion or a wrong call. Build one standing rule: ask the sample-size and base-rate question before the decision, not after the surprise.
3. **Base-Rate Reader (21–26):** You already check sample size and expect regression most of the time. Strengthen it by naming your check out loud so your team learns to ask the same question before you have to.
4. **Statistical Operator (27–32):** You run a visible, named routine for checking numbers before reacting and protect it under pressure. Teach one direct report your sample-size and base-rate questions so the routine survives when you are off-shift.
