# Self-Assessment — How Much Are Ease, Anchors, and Availability Steering You?

Answer for how you **actually** lead this month (A=1 … D=4). Be honest—this is diagnostic, not a performance review.

--- SECTION 1: QUESTIONS ---

**Q1.** In a huddle, when someone throws out a rough number before the real report is open, you:
- A) Let it stand and build the discussion around it
- B) Notice it might be a guess but rarely say so out loud
- C) Ask for the actual source before the team keeps estimating
- D) Have a standing rule that no number counts until the source is on screen

**Q2.** When you are the first to name a figure in a negotiation, estimate, or target-setting conversation, you:
- A) Do not think about how much your own number will pull others
- B) Notice afterward that people tended to agree with your first guess
- C) Deliberately hold back your own guess until others have weighed in
- D) Actively plan the order of who speaks first to avoid one number anchoring the room

**Q3.** After a vivid, recent incident (injury, viral complaint, dramatic near-miss), your read on how often it happens is:
- A) Driven mostly by how sharp that one memory still feels
- B) Somewhat inflated, but you rarely check it against the actual rate
- C) Checked against the actual count over recent months before you react
- D) Tracked on a running log specifically so memory does not distort the rate

**Q4.** A report or message that reads smoothly and looks polished, you:
- A) Trust more than a rougher one, without noticing why
- B) Trust more, and only sometimes catch yourself doing it
- C) Separate how it reads from what evidence actually backs it
- D) Have a habit of asking "what backs this beyond how clean it looks?" before acting

**Q5.** A case that closely resembles a pattern you have seen before (a "type" of return, employee, or defect), you:
- A) Judge mainly by how well it matches the pattern
- B) Lean on the resemblance, then sometimes check the numbers after
- C) Check the actual base rate for that pattern before deciding
- D) Always weigh resemblance against a known base rate before acting

**Q6.** A new hire or teammate who reminds you of someone from your past who caused problems, you:
- A) Treat with more suspicion than the evidence in front of you supports
- B) Notice the association but let it color your read anyway
- C) Actively separate the resemblance from this person's actual record
- D) Flag the association to yourself as a bias risk and judge only on this record

**Q7.** When you try to recall how many of your recent calls were right, you:
- A) Assume the ones that come to mind easily represent your real track record
- B) Sense this might be skewed but do not check it
- C) Look back at an actual log before drawing a conclusion
- D) Keep a simple running record specifically so memory cannot distort your self-view

**Q8.** Your team's floor culture around catching these shortcuts (ease, anchors, availability, resemblance) is:
- A) Nobody names them; the first number or the strongest impression wins
- B) Individuals sometimes catch themselves, with no shared language
- C) Catching a shortcut out loud is respected and welcomed
- D) The team has a visible ritual (checklist, phrase, log) that protects the check even under deadline pressure

--- SECTION 2: SCORING ---

Score A=1, B=2, C=3, D=4. Total range 8–32.

--- SECTION 3: PROFILES ---

1. **Shortcut-Driven Operator (8–14):** Ease, anchors, availability, and resemblance are steering most of your calls, often without your noticing. Useful under real time pressure; risky whenever the fluent, first-heard, memorable, or resemblance-matched answer happens to be wrong. Pick one recurring call and add a ten-second source check before you commit.
2. **Occasional Bias-Catcher (15–20):** You catch one of the four sometimes, usually after a call has already gone sideways. Build one standing rule: name the specific shortcut — ease, anchor, availability, or resemblance — before you act, not after.
3. **Deliberate Checker (21–26):** You already separate fluency, first numbers, vivid memory, and resemblance from the actual evidence most of the time. Strengthen it by naming your check out loud so your team learns to flag it with you.
4. **Bias-Aware System Owner (27–32):** You run a visible, named process for catching all four shortcuts and protect it under pressure. Teach one direct report your source-check habit so it survives when you are not on shift.
