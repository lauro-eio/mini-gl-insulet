# Two Speeds of Judgment: What the Evidence Says About Snap Calls and Slow Checks

Frontline leaders make dozens of calls a shift: pull a picker off task, wave a shipment through, escalate a jam versus clear it themselves. Most of these calls are made fast, and most of the time fast is right. Cognitive science has spent decades mapping when fast judgment holds up and when it quietly fails.

## Two systems, one brain

Researchers describe two broad modes of thinking. One is automatic, associative, and nearly effortless: it recognizes a familiar pattern and answers before a person has consciously "decided" anything. The other is slower, deliberate, and effortful: it checks assumptions, weighs alternatives, and can override the first answer. Psychologists label these System 1 and System 2 processing (Kahneman & Tversky, 1974; Evans, 2008). Neither system is superior. Each fits different conditions.

## When fast is right

Gary Klein's field studies of firefighters, nurses, and other high-stakes operators found that experienced practitioners often make strong snap decisions by matching a live situation to patterns they have seen before—a process called recognition-primed decision-making (Klein, 1993). Kahneman and Klein (2009), in a paper written jointly to resolve their long-standing disagreement on this point, concluded that fast intuition earns trust when two conditions hold: the environment is regular enough to contain learnable patterns, and the person has had enough repeated practice with feedback to learn them. A supervisor who has run hundreds of similar shift handoffs has earned fast judgment on shift handoffs specifically.

## When fast fails

The same regularity that makes intuition reliable in familiar territory makes it unreliable outside it. Kahneman and Tversky's foundational research catalogued specific ways fast judgment misfires under uncertainty: anchoring on the first number offered, overweighting a vivid recent case, and mistaking a confident feeling for an accurate one (Kahneman & Tversky, 1974). Stanovich and West (2000) found these errors persist even among capable, motivated people—the issue is rarely intelligence; it is which system got engaged. Croskerry's work on diagnostic reasoning in medicine, a field where fast pattern-matching and rare, costly misses coexist daily, found that practitioners who ran a brief structured second pass caught errors that intuition alone missed, particularly on atypical or high-stakes cases (Croskerry, 2009).

## The floor version of the switch

For a frontline leader, the practical question is not "should I trust my gut," but "does this situation match the conditions under which my gut has actually been trained." Three conditions predict when a deliberate check earns back its cost in time:

1. **Novelty**—this scenario does not closely match ones handled repeatedly before.
2. **Stakes**—a wrong call is costly, hard to reverse, or touches safety.
3. **Conflicting signals**—instinct and the data in front of a person disagree.

When none of the three are present, fast judgment is typically efficient and correct. When even one is strongly present, the evidence favors a brief, structured pause—not paralysis, but a deliberate second pass before committing.

## What this means for supervisors

Build a habit of naming which system is driving a call, out loud when useful, before acting on high-novelty or high-stakes decisions. The goal is not to slow every decision down; that would make a leader the bottleneck. The goal is to protect deliberate checking specifically for the calls where fast judgment has not yet earned trust.
