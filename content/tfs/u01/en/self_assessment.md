# Self-Assessment — Are You Running on Autopilot or Analysis?

Answer for how you **actually** lead this month (A=1 … D=4). Be honest—this is diagnostic, not a performance review.

--- SECTION 1: QUESTIONS ---

**Q1.** When a floor issue looks like one you have solved before, you usually:
- A) Act immediately, no second look
- B) Act, then double-check later if something feels off
- C) Pause briefly to confirm the pattern actually matches before acting
- D) Run a quick structured check whenever stakes are meaningful, even if the case looks familiar

**Q2.** When a decision is costly or hard to reverse (safety override, line stoppage, escalation), you:
- A) Trust your gut and move fast regardless of stakes
- B) Slow down only if someone else raises a concern
- C) Deliberately slow down and verify before committing
- D) Follow a standing rule that high-stakes calls always get a second check

**Q3.** When your instinct and the data in front of you disagree, you:
- A) Go with instinct—it is usually right
- B) Notice the conflict but often act on instinct anyway
- C) Stop and resolve the conflict before deciding
- D) Treat any instinct-versus-data conflict as an automatic trigger for the slow process

**Q4.** A less experienced teammate flags that your fast call might be wrong. You:
- A) Dismiss it—you have done this a hundred times
- B) Half-listen, then proceed with your original call
- C) Pause and check what they saw, even if it costs time
- D) Thank them and treat it as a formal check trigger, not a nuisance

**Q5.** Under pressure from above to move fast, you:
- A) Skip checks entirely to keep pace
- B) Skip checks on some calls, keep them on others without a clear rule
- C) Protect the check on high-stakes calls and speed up only the routine ones
- D) Explain to leadership which calls get checked and why, before the pressure hits

**Q6.** Your sense of when a floor situation is "new" versus "routine" is:
- A) You do not distinguish—every familiar-looking case gets the same response
- B) You notice sometimes, usually after something has already gone wrong
- C) You actively compare the situation to ones you have truly handled before deciding
- D) You check it against named criteria (novelty, stakes, conflicting signals)

**Q7.** When a fast call you made turns out right, you:
- A) Take it as proof fast judgment always works here
- B) Feel good about it but do not examine why it worked
- C) Note what made this case genuinely familiar, to sharpen the pattern next time
- D) Log it as evidence for or against trusting that pattern next time

**Q8.** Your team's floor culture around pausing to check is:
- A) Pausing is seen as weakness or slowness
- B) Pausing happens informally, with no shared language
- C) Pausing on flagged risks is respected and expected
- D) The team has a visible ritual (checklist, phrase, signal) that protects the pause even under deadline pressure

--- SECTION 2: SCORING ---

Score A=1, B=2, C=3, D=4. Total range 8–32.

--- SECTION 3: PROFILES ---

1. **Instinct-Only Operator (8–14):** You run almost everything on fast judgment, including calls that have not earned that trust. Useful under real time pressure; risky on novel or costly calls. Pick one recurring high-stakes decision and add a ten-second named check before you act on it.
2. **Occasional Checker (15–20):** You slow down sometimes, usually after something has already gone sideways. Build one standing rule: name the trigger—novelty, stakes, or conflict—before the mistake, not after.
3. **Deliberate Reader (21–26):** You already distinguish familiar from unfamiliar patterns and pause accordingly. Strengthen it by naming your criteria out loud so your team learns to flag conflicts to you early.
4. **Systems Analyst (27–32):** You run a visible, named process for switching speeds and protect it under pressure. Teach one direct report your novelty, stakes, and conflict criteria so the ritual survives when you are not on shift.
