# Case 2 — Aisha's Snap Call on a Safety Override

## Opening

Aisha coordinates a warehouse dock crew of nine. A recurring nuisance alarm on dock door 3 trips whenever a pallet jack passes too close to the sensor, even with no real obstruction. Crew members routinely override it and move on—Aisha has approved that override herself at least twice a week for a year with no incident.

## Context

A rush truck is loading against a hard cutoff. Dock door 3 trips again. The crew lead radios Aisha for the override, the same request she has granted without hesitation dozens of times. This time, the alarm log on her handheld shows a slightly different error code than the usual nuisance trip—one she has not seen paired with this door before. She has forty seconds before the crew moves ahead on habit alone, override or not, because they have learned that Aisha always says yes.

Her floor's overrides over the past quarter, tracked in the shift log, look like this:

| Week | Overrides granted | Overrides later found to mask a real fault |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1–8 | 15 total | 0 |
| 9 | 2 | 0 |
| 10 | 2 | 1 (different error code, same door) |

## Decision point

Aisha can:

1. Approve the override immediately, consistent with a year of correct calls on this exact door.
2. Radio a hold, walk to the door, and read the sensor housing herself before deciding—delaying the load by a few minutes.
3. Approve the override for now to protect the cutoff, and flag the door for an inspection before the next shift, accepting the risk in between.

## Discussion questions

1. What single detail turned a routine override into a case worth a second look, and would the crew have noticed it without Aisha?
2. How should Aisha weigh a rush cutoff against a low-probability, high-consequence safety risk?
3. Design a floor cue that would let any crew member flag "this override looks slightly different" without slowing down every normal override.
