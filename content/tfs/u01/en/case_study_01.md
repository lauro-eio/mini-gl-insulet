# Case 1 — Marcus and the Line Stoppage Call

## Opening

Marcus supervises the second shift on a small-parts assembly line at a mid-sized manufacturer. He has run this line for three years and can diagnose most jams by sound alone. Ninety percent of stoppages trace back to one cause: a sensor at station 4 drifting out of alignment. His fix takes ninety seconds and he has done it hundreds of times without needing to check anything else.

## Context

Tonight the line stops at 11:40 p.m. with the same alarm code Marcus has seen a hundred times before. His hand is already reaching for the sensor reset. A newer technician, Devon, mentions that maintenance swapped a different component upstream two nights ago—something Marcus was off-shift for and never heard about. The alarm looks identical to the usual sensor drift, but the underlying cause this time could be entirely different: a mounting bracket from the new component, not the sensor at all.

Resetting the sensor when the real problem is the bracket would clear the alarm temporarily and let the line restart—then fail again within the hour, likely during the busiest stretch of the shift, and with a small chance of a part misalignment reaching packout undetected.

## Decision point

Marcus can:

1. Reset the sensor immediately, the way he always does, and get the line moving in ninety seconds.
2. Take four extra minutes to check the maintenance log and inspect the new component before touching anything.
3. Reset the sensor now to restart the line, and quietly schedule a bracket inspection for the next scheduled pause—accepting the small risk of a repeat stoppage or an undetected defect in between.

## Discussion questions

1. What made tonight's alarm look identical to the usual case, and what evidence actually distinguishes them?
2. Which of the three conditions—novelty, stakes, or conflicting signals—applies here, and how strongly?
3. If Marcus chooses option 2, how should he explain the four-minute delay to a shift lead who is watching the line-down clock?
