# Case 1 — Marisol and the New Hire Who Froze

## Opening

Marisol supervises a receiving dock crew of six at a distribution center. She has a reputation for turning new hires into competent operators fast—usually within two weeks, a new person can run the scanner, the lift, and the intake queue without supervision. She has never had a serious incident with a new hire, and she credits her tight, checklist-driven onboarding.

## Context

In his third week, a new hire named Deshawn is working the intake queue when a pallet arrives flagged for a safety hold—torn shrink wrap, contents visibly shifted, genuinely unstable. The correct response is simple: stop, tag it, call it in. Deshawn has been trained on the exact procedure. But when the moment actually arrives, his hands start shaking, his mind goes blank, and instead of stopping he tries to quietly restack the pallet himself so it "looks fine," nearly causing exactly the kind of fall the hold procedure exists to prevent. A teammate catches it in time.

Afterward, Deshawn tells Marisol he knew the procedure cold. He just does not remember deciding to skip it—one second he was reading the tag, the next he was already restacking, hands moving faster than his thinking. Marisol realizes that in two weeks of onboarding, she taught Deshawn exactly what to do and never once taught him what to do with the jolt that shows up right when the procedure matters most.

## Decision point

Marisol can:

1. Retrain Deshawn on the hold procedure again, more firmly, and treat this as a compliance gap to close with repetition.
2. Add a standing two-minute check-in to her onboarding buddy pairing—asking every new hire, each shift for two weeks, what came up today that felt bigger than the moment—starting with Deshawn now and every new hire after him.
3. Quietly move Deshawn to a lower-stakes station for a few months until he "settles in," without changing how she onboards anyone else.

## Discussion questions

1. What did Deshawn actually lack—knowledge of the procedure, or something else—and how does that change what Marisol should fix?
2. If Marisol chooses option 2, what makes a two-minute check-in different from just retraining the procedure again?
3. What would tell Marisol, a month from now, whether the new check-in is actually happening for every new hire, or has quietly become something she only remembers to do with hires she likes?
