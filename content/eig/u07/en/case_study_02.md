# Case 2 — Grant and the Peer Check After the Near-Miss

## Opening

Grant leads a maintenance crew of nine at a mid-sized plant. Two days ago, a teammate, Priya, had a close call—a guard came loose on a machine she was servicing, and for a half-second she thought she was about to lose a finger. She was not hurt. She has said almost nothing about it since, and has been unusually quiet on the floor.

## Context

Grant's plant has a formal incident report process, which Priya completed the same day, and a mandatory safety briefing where the near-miss was discussed as a procedural failure—the guard latch design—with an action item to inspect the others. Nobody has asked Priya, directly and privately, how she is doing with what happened to her, as opposed to what happened to the machine. Grant notices she has stopped joining the crew's usual joking-around at lunch, and he is not sure whether to bring it up, worried he will make it a bigger deal than she wants it to be, or worse, imply she cannot handle her job.

## Decision point

Grant can:

1. Say nothing directly—trust that the formal incident report and safety briefing already covered it, and let Priya come to him if she needs to.
2. Pull Priya aside for a brief, low-key check-in—not a debrief of the incident, just asking how she is doing since Tuesday—and make this a standing practice for anyone involved in a near-miss going forward.
3. Raise it as a topic in the next all-crew huddle, asking the group generally how everyone is feeling about near-misses lately, without naming Priya specifically.

## Discussion questions

1. What is the difference between the formal incident report Priya already filed and the kind of check-in option 2 describes—and why might both be necessary?
2. What is the risk if Grant does nothing, versus the risk if his check-in feels like an evaluation of whether Priya can still do her job?
3. If Grant builds a standing peer-check practice, who should own it when Grant is the one involved in the next near-miss?
