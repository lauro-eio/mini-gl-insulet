# The New Hire I Almost Set Up to Fail

I trained Theo the way I train everyone: two days shadowing a veteran, a checklist of procedures, sign-off on the equipment, done. By his second week he could run the line as well as anyone with three months in. I was proud of that turnaround. I did not think about what I had not taught him until it almost cost us.

A supplier issue triggered an unplanned stop on Theo's line during his third week. Nothing dangerous—just a jammed feed and a red light nobody had warned him about. But Theo froze, then panicked, hitting buttons that made it worse before a teammate caught it. Afterward he told me, quietly, that he had felt his heart pounding and his hands shaking and had no idea what to do with that feeling except keep pressing buttons until something worked.

I realized I had handed Theo a checklist for the machine and nothing for himself. Every other person on my crew had picked up, informally, over months, some version of noticing a spike and taking half a second before reacting. Theo had been on the floor three weeks. Nobody had ever named it in front of him, so he had nothing to reach for when his body did exactly what bodies do under a sudden alarm.

I changed how I onboard now. Every new hire gets paired with a buddy for the first two weeks, and the buddy's job is not just showing them the machine—it is a two-minute check-in at the end of each shift: what came up today that felt bigger than the moment, and what did you do with it. I do the same thing myself in the pre-shift huddle once a week, naming a moment from my own week out loud so the newest person on the crew sees it is not a special skill reserved for veterans.

I still run the same equipment checklist. I have not softened anything about how fast I expect a new hire to get productive. What changed is that I stopped treating the emotional side of the job as something people would just pick up eventually, the way I did, by accident, over years. Theo did not fail that day because he lacked skill on the machine. He failed because nobody had built the other half of onboarding—the part where you learn what to do with the jolt—into his first three weeks. That gap is on the trainer, not the trainee, and it is a gap any supervisor can close before the next new hire's first hard moment finds it for them.
