# Why Training Fades: What the Evidence Says About Making a Skill Stick

Frontline leaders send a crew through a workshop, watch the room nod along, and then watch the same old reactions show up on the floor three weeks later—as if the day never happened. That is not a training problem specific to any one program. It is one of the best-documented findings in the science of workplace learning.

## The transfer problem has a name

Baldwin and Ford (1988) reviewed decades of research and gave this pattern a name: the transfer problem. A skill learned in a room does not automatically generalize to the job or survive over time. What determines whether it does is not how good the training day felt, but three things working together afterward: the trainee's own motivation to use it, how the training was designed, and—critically—what the work environment does with it once the trainee is back on shift.

## What actually predicts whether it sticks

Grossman and Salas (2011), reviewing the transfer literature since Baldwin and Ford, narrowed this down further: the strongest predictors of a skill surviving contact with the job are a supportive work environment, real opportunity to practice it, and follow-up after the initial training. Salas, Tannenbaum, Kraiger, and Smith-Jentsch (2012) reach a similar conclusion from a different angle: training is not a single event, it is a system, and what happens before and after the session matters as much as the session itself. Neither review found that a well-delivered workshop, on its own, reliably produces lasting change on the floor.

## Onboarding is the highest-leverage moment, not an afterthought

Bauer (2010), summarizing onboarding research for the SHRM Foundation, found that new hires who receive only compliance and task training—without deliberate attention to team culture and connection—adjust more slowly and are more likely to leave within the first year. The habits a new hire builds in the first weeks are disproportionately durable. A skill introduced later, once someone already has a working, if incomplete, way of handling pressure, has to compete with habits that are already set.

## The skill only survives if it is safe to use out loud

Edmondson (1999), studying learning behavior in fifty-one work teams, found that team psychological safety—the shared belief that speaking up will not be punished—was the strongest predictor of whether a team engaged in the small, visible learning behaviors that let a skill compound over time: asking a question, naming an error, flagging a concern. A leader can teach a skill perfectly and still lose it if naming a spike out loud reads as weakness on that particular floor.

## Peer-delivered check-ins, tested where it counts

Greenberg, Langston, Iversen, and Wessely (2010) ran a cluster-randomized trial of a peer-delivered check-in program across twelve Royal Navy vessels following potentially traumatic events. The program did not significantly shift measured psychological health scores over the study period—incident rates were low across the trial—but organizational functioning was modestly better where it ran, and the authors found no evidence of harm. The honest reading is not that peer check-ins are a guaranteed fix; it is that a brief, peer-delivered, non-clinical conversation after a hard moment is a low-risk way to keep a team's culture from going quiet.

## What this means for supervisors

Taken together, this research points to the same conclusion from four directions: a skill taught once in a room fades unless it is deliberately built into moments the team already passes through—how a new hire is brought on, what gets modeled in the daily huddle, and how the team checks on each other after something goes wrong. None of those moments require a special curriculum. They require a leader who decides, in advance, that this is where the skill lives now.
