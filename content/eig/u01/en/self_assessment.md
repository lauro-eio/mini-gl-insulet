# Self-Assessment — Do You Notice the Surge or Just React to It?

Answer for how you **actually** lead this month (A=1 … D=4). Be honest—this is diagnostic, not a performance review.

--- SECTION 1: QUESTIONS ---

**Q1.** When you feel a sudden jolt of anger or frustration during a floor conflict, you usually:
- A) Say what's on your mind right away
- B) Say something, then regret the tone later
- C) Notice the jolt and pause briefly before responding
- D) Name the specific feeling to yourself before you decide how to respond

**Q2.** When a peer or teammate challenges your call in front of the team, you:
- A) Defend yourself immediately, tone and all
- B) Feel the sting but usually respond in kind anyway
- C) Notice the sting and choose your words deliberately
- D) Treat any public challenge as an automatic cue to pause before responding

**Q3.** When a safety incident or near-miss triggers a spike of fear or adrenaline, you:
- A) Act on the urgency immediately, whatever it tells you to do
- B) Notice the spike but usually let it set the pace anyway
- C) Notice the spike and deliberately slow your next instruction
- D) Follow a standing rule that any fear spike triggers a beat before the next instruction

**Q4.** Your face or voice visibly shows tension before you have said anything. You:
- A) Do not think about how it lands on the team
- B) Notice sometimes, usually after someone reacts to your tone
- C) Actively check what you are broadcasting before you speak
- D) Treat visible tension as information your team is already using, and adjust for it

**Q5.** Under pressure to respond fast, you:
- A) Let the first reaction out, no filter
- B) Filter some reactions, not others, without a clear rule
- C) Protect a brief pause on high-stakes moments and let routine ones move fast
- D) Explain to your team, before pressure hits, which moments get a pause and why

**Q6.** Your sense of when a reaction is bigger than the situation warrants is:
- A) You do not distinguish—every jolt gets acted on the same way
- B) You notice sometimes, usually after the reaction has already shown
- C) You actively compare the size of the feeling to the size of the moment before acting
- D) You check it against named signs (intensity, a call attached, visible spread)

**Q7.** When a fast, emotional reaction of yours turns out to be right, you:
- A) Take it as proof your gut is always right
- B) Feel good about it but do not examine why it worked
- C) Note what made this reaction genuinely proportionate, to sharpen it next time
- D) Log it as evidence for or against trusting that reaction next time

**Q8.** Your team's floor culture around naming a feeling before reacting is:
- A) Naming a feeling is seen as oversharing or weakness
- B) It happens informally, with no shared language
- C) Naming a feeling before a tense call is respected and expected
- D) The team has a visible ritual (phrase, signal, pause) that protects the beat even under deadline pressure

--- SECTION 2: SCORING ---

Score A=1, B=2, C=3, D=4. Total range 8–32.

--- SECTION 3: PROFILES ---

1. **Reactive Responder (8–14):** You act on almost every surge as it lands, including ones your team will remember for the wrong reasons. Useful when speed genuinely matters; risky when intensity or stakes are high. Pick one recurring trigger and add a five-second silent label before you speak.
2. **Occasional Namer (15–20):** You notice the signal sometimes, usually after your tone has already landed on someone. Build one standing rule: name the trigger—intensity, a call attached, or visible spread—before you speak, not after.
3. **Deliberate Reader (21–26):** You already distinguish a proportionate reaction from an outsized one and pause accordingly. Strengthen it by naming your criteria out loud so your team learns to give you the beat without being asked.
4. **Signal Steward (27–32):** You run a visible, named ritual for noticing and labeling surges and protect it under pressure. Teach one direct report your triggers so the ritual survives when you are not on shift.
