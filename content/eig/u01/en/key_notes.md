# Key Notes — Reading the Signal Before the Call

**Unit goal:** Treat emotional surges as evolved signals to notice and regulate—not ignore—so team calls stay deliberate under pressure.

## Core ideas

- **Signals, not glitches:** anger, fear, and other surges are inherited response programs, shaped to solve recurring problems fast—not personal failures of professionalism (Tooby & Cosmides, 1990; Nesse, 1990).
- **The fast circuit fires first:** a bodily reaction can arrive before the slower, deliberate part of the brain has finished evaluating what happened (LeDoux, 2000). You cannot out-think the first spike—but you can decide what happens next.
- **Your team already reads it:** facial and vocal signals are visible the moment they appear, across cultures (Ekman & Friesen, 1971). Ignoring your own surge does not hide it from the crew.
- **Three conditions for a deliberate beat:** intensity mismatch, a call attached to the moment, and visible spread to the team.
- **A brief label beats both extremes:** neither suppressing the signal nor reacting on it immediately serves a floor call well.

## Frontline leader checklist

1. Notice the physical signal (tight chest, raised voice, clenched jaw) before you notice the thought.
2. Put one word on it—silently or out loud—before you speak or act: "defensive anger," "startle fear," "frustration."
3. Ask one question instead of making one statement when the surge is running.
4. Keep a visible team cue for "give me a beat" that does not read as weakness.
5. After the shift, note one surge you named well and one you missed—both sharpen the skill.

## Reflection

Which recurring floor moment reliably triggers a surge in you, and what would naming it one beat earlier change about what you say next?
