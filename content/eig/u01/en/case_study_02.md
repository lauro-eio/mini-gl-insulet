# Case 2 — Devon and the Near-Miss on the Dock

## Opening

Devon coordinates a warehouse dock crew of eight. A forklift operator clips a stack of pallets while backing up near a walkway; nobody is hurt, but a crew member had been standing two feet from where the pallets fell. Devon feels the fear spike hit before he has even finished turning around—tight chest, narrowed focus, an urge to shout at the operator right now, in front of everyone.

## Context

The operator, visibly rattled, is waiting for Devon's reaction. The rest of the crew has stopped moving and is watching. A truck at the dock has a hard cutoff in eighteen minutes, and the crew lead is already asking whether they can resume loading. Devon's instinct pulls two ways at once: explode at the operator to underline how serious this was, or wave it off fast to protect the cutoff and keep morale from cratering.

Neither instinct, acted on immediately, addresses what actually needs to happen: checking the operator, checking the walkway sightline, and deciding what the crew needs to hear in the next two minutes.

## Decision point

Devon can:

1. React immediately—raise his voice at the operator on the spot to underline the severity, then get back to loading.
2. Take thirty seconds of visible silence, name the fear spike to himself, check the operator and the crew member, then decide what to say.
3. Say nothing about the near-miss beyond "everyone okay?", resume loading to protect the cutoff, and address it fully at the end of the shift.

## Discussion questions

1. What would Devon's face and voice already be broadcasting to the crew in the first ten seconds, whether he intends it or not?
2. How should Devon weigh the visible spread of his own fear response against the crew's need for a steady voice right now?
3. Design a floor cue Devon's crew could use so that anyone—operator included—can signal "give me a beat" after a near-miss, without it reading as blame.
