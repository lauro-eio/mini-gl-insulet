# Case 1 — Renata and the Line Handoff Confrontation

## Opening

Renata supervises the day shift on a small-parts assembly line at a mid-sized manufacturer. She has run this line for four years and prides herself on a clean handoff to the next shift—no surprises, no blame. She knows her jaw tightens fast when she feels accused in front of a crew.

## Context

At shift change, the incoming supervisor, Callum, radios in front of both crews that a quality hold just got called on a batch Renata's team packed an hour earlier—"another one from your line," he says, loud enough for six people to hear. Renata feels the jolt immediately: heat in her face, a sentence already forming about the two holds his crew let through last week. She has maybe forty-five seconds before both crews start drawing their own conclusions about who is at fault.

A newer packer on Renata's crew, Dara, quietly mentions that the same defect code showed up on a supplier alert posted that morning—one Renata has not read yet.

## Decision point

Renata can:

1. Respond immediately, matching Callum's tone and defending her crew on the radio in front of everyone.
2. Say nothing for several seconds, silently name what she is feeling, then ask Callum one question about what he actually saw before responding.
3. Wave Callum off for now, tell both crews to keep moving, and address the hold and the tone privately after the floor clears—accepting that the public accusation sits unanswered in the meantime.

## Discussion questions

1. What in Renata's body told her the reaction was bigger than the moment warranted, and how could she use that signal instead of ignoring it?
2. Which of the three conditions—intensity mismatch, a call attached, or visible spread—applies most strongly here?
3. If Renata chooses option 2, what does she say in the ten seconds after noticing the jolt, and how does that differ from what she originally wanted to say?
