# Strategic Role-Play — Securing a Trust-Repair Norm After a Public Mistake

**Duration:** 40–50 minutes
**Purpose:** Practice securing buy-in for a named, criteria-based trust-repair practice after a mistake became visible to more than one shift.

## Four roles

1. **Supervisor (protagonist):** Wants approval for a standing practice: any trust break gets diagnosed by which leg wobbled—ability, benevolence, or integrity—before a repair is chosen and logged.
2. **Speed-focused ops manager (antagonist):** Measured on throughput; sees repair conversations as lost time and calls the practice "overthinking a mistake that's already fixed."
3. **Quality or safety analyst (skeptic):** Supports the idea in principle but needs named criteria for what counts as a trust break versus routine friction, plus a clear time box for how long a repair should take.
4. **Peer supervisor (ally or swing):** Will support the practice if it does not turn every small disagreement into a formal repair process, and if it does not quietly become a way to avoid direct conversations.

## Conflict thesis

Can the supervisor protect a named, diagnosis-first repair practice for genuine trust breaks without looking soft to the ops manager or vague to the analyst?

## Success look-fors

- Named legs of trust (ability, benevolence, integrity), not a blanket "let's talk about feelings"
- A clear line between routine task friction and an actual trust break
- A fixed time box for repair, agreed in advance
- A visible record of when the practice was used and what it changed

## Debrief prompts

1. Which argument landed with the ops manager versus the analyst?
2. How did the supervisor turn "diagnose before repairing" into language that reads as risk control, not softness?
3. What would cause the practice to quietly disappear during the next crunch week—and who owns preventing that?
