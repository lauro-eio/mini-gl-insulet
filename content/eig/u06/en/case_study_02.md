# Case 2 — Dario and the Covered Defect

## Opening

Dario operates a press station on an assembly line supervised by Renee. Midway through a shift, he notices a defect on a run he already packed and sealed for shipment. Reporting it would mean stopping the line, reopening a sealed batch, and missing the shift's output target. Dario decides not to say anything, hoping the defect is minor enough not to matter.

## Context

The defect surfaces a week later when the customer returns the batch. Renee traces it back to Dario's station and confirms, from timestamped logs, that he clocked the run as clean well after he would have seen the flaw. When she raises it with him, Dario apologizes and says he "didn't think it was a big deal." Renee finds herself more unsettled by the silence than by the defect itself—she now reads every report Dario submits with the assumption there may be something he chose not to mention.

## Decision point

Renee can:

1. Accept the apology, remind Dario of the reporting standard, and treat it as resolved once the conversation is over.
2. Name directly that the issue is not the defect but the choice not to report it, and set a specific, time-boxed way for Dario to rebuild that his reports can be trusted without double-checking.
3. Say nothing further, but quietly start re-verifying Dario's output herself going forward, without telling him that is what changed.

## Discussion questions

1. Why does an apology that would likely resolve an honest mistake fall short here—what is actually broken, and what would prove it is fixed?
2. What is the cost to Renee, and to the rest of the line, of choosing option 3 instead of naming the issue directly?
3. Design a floor norm that would make it safer for someone in Dario's position to report a defect immediately, even when it costs the shift's output target.
