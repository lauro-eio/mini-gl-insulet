# Task or Trust: What the Evidence Says About Conflict and Repair on a Floor Team

Two supervisors disagree about how to sequence a rush order. Thirty seconds later, one of them is not just defending a schedule—she is defending whether her judgment can be trusted at all. Decades of research on conflict and trust in teams explain why that shift happens so fast, and what actually repairs it once it does.

## Two conflicts, not one

Jehn (1995) draws a sharp line between task conflict—disagreement about what to do, how to sequence work, which method is right—and relationship conflict—friction about who someone is, personal tension, distrust. A later meta-analysis by De Dreu and Weingart (2003) found that task conflict, kept at a moderate level, does not reliably hurt team performance; relationship conflict consistently does, dragging down both performance and satisfaction. The problem on a floor is not that disagreement exists. It is that a disagreement about the work routinely slides into a disagreement about the person inside the same exchange, without anyone naming that the subject just changed.

## Trust rests on three legs

Mayer, Davis, and Schoorman's (1995) integrative model of trust describes it as resting on three separate judgments one person makes about another: **ability** (can they actually do this job), **benevolence** (do they mean well toward me, or are they out for themselves), and **integrity** (do they keep their word and tell the truth, even when it costs them). A supervisor who snaps "I don't trust your count" after a scheduling disagreement may be reacting to any one of these three—and treating them as interchangeable is exactly where repair attempts go wrong.

## Which leg wobbled changes the repair

Kim, Ferrin, Cooper, and Dirks (2004) tested how people respond to two different kinds of trust violations. For a **competence-based** violation—an honest mistake, a missed defect, a misjudged deadline—an apology paired with a concrete fix tends to restore trust reasonably well; people forgive a capability gap once it is named and addressed. For an **integrity-based** violation—someone hid a mistake, broke a promise, or was caught in a lie—an apology alone tends to land far weaker than the person offering it expects. Rebuilding that kind of trust requires consistent, verifiable behavior sustained over time, not a well-worded conversation in the moment. Treating a lie like a slip, or a slip like a lie, wastes the repair attempt on the wrong problem.

## Psychological safety keeps task conflict from turning personal

Edmondson (1999) found that teams with higher psychological safety—where members believe they can speak up, disagree, or admit an error without being punished for it—show more learning behavior: more error reporting, more open disagreement about the work, faster correction. That safety is what allows a genuine task disagreement to be raised and settled without either party reading it as a character attack, and what allows a real mistake to surface early, while it is still small and cheap to fix.

## What this means for a floor leader

Two moves, before responding to a heated moment: first, name whether the friction in front of you is actually about the task—the count, the sequence, the call—or whether it has already become a question of trust. Second, if it is trust, name which leg is wobbling: ability, benevolence, or integrity. A supervisor who treats every disagreement as a personal attack will provoke relationship conflict where none needed to exist. A supervisor who treats every trust break as a training gap—apologizing and moving on when the real issue was honesty—will find the apology does not hold, and the crew notices that it didn't.
