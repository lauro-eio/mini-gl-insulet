# Case 1 — Priya and the Missed Count

## Opening

Priya leads inventory close-out on the evening shift at a distribution center. She has run the close-out process for two years without a serious error and is known for catching other people's mistakes before they leave the building. Tonight, rushing to finish before a truck cutoff, she signs off a count that is one pallet short.

## Context

The shortfall is not discovered until the morning shift's receiving lead, Jordan, tries to reconcile the manifest and comes up short by exactly the amount Priya's count missed. Jordan has to hold a truck for forty minutes while the floor searches for the missing pallet, which turns out to have been mis-scanned, not lost. Jordan is frustrated and says, in front of two other leads, "I don't know if I can trust your counts anymore." Priya feels the accusation as bigger than the mistake—she has never missed a count like this before, and Jordan's comment sounds like it is about more than one bad night.

## Decision point

Priya can:

1. Apologize immediately and explain exactly how the miscount happened, assuming the explanation itself will settle Jordan's concern.
2. Ask Jordan directly what specifically is making him unsure—the mistake itself, or something about how it played out—before deciding what kind of repair to offer.
3. Downplay it as a one-off and point out her two-year track record, expecting the record to speak for itself without further conversation.

## Discussion questions

1. Is Jordan's comment most likely about Priya's ability to count correctly, or something else entirely—and how would Priya know which one it is without asking?
2. If this is a competence-based trust question, what repair actually rebuilds it, beyond an apology?
3. What would change about Priya's response if Jordan had said "I don't know if I can trust what you tell me" instead of "trust your counts"?
