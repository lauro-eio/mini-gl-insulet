# The Feedback I Almost Gave Angry

I had a 1:1 booked with one of my packers, Theo, about a missed count on his line for the third week running. Twenty minutes before the meeting, a different problem landed on me: my own shift lead had missed a handoff and left me covering two stations short-handed for an hour. By the time I sat down across from Theo, I was already carrying something.

I almost let it out on him. I had a whole opening line ready about "this being the third time" and how "I need this to stop," and the tone behind it was not really about Theo's count. It was about the hour I had just spent short two stations, with nobody warning me it was coming.

I caught it because I have started doing one thing before any coaching conversation that matters: naming the specific feeling, out loud in my head, before I open my mouth. Not "I'm upset." Something more exact. I sat there for maybe five seconds and landed on it: "residual frustration, not really about Theo." Once I had the actual word, I could hear how much of my opening line belonged to the handoff, not the count.

So I changed what I said. Instead of "this is the third time and I need it to stop," I asked him what was actually happening on his end during that window. It turned out he had been covering for a teammate out sick and had not flagged it, assuming it would sort itself out. That is a real coaching conversation—about coverage, about when to flag a gap—not a conversation about my morning.

If I had opened with the angry version, Theo would have walked away defending himself against a tone that had nothing to do with him, and I would have missed the actual thing worth coaching: that he needs to flag a coverage gap instead of quietly absorbing it. The feedback would have technically happened. It would not have been usable.

I am not trying to arrive at every 1:1 feeling nothing. That is not realistic, and it is not the goal. What changed is what I do with five seconds before I speak: name the specific feeling, check whether it actually belongs to the person across from me, and only then start the conversation. My crew does not need a supervisor with no feelings walking into a coaching conversation. They need one who has checked whose feelings are actually in the room before opening their mouth.
