# Self-Assessment — Do You Coach From a Named State or a Raw One?

Answer for how you **actually** coach this month (A=1 … D=4). Be honest—this is diagnostic, not a performance review.

--- SECTION 1: QUESTIONS ---

**Q1.** Right before a coaching conversation about a real problem, you usually:
- A) Walk in and say what's on your mind
- B) Notice you're carrying something, but start talking anyway
- C) Pause briefly and check what you're feeling before you open your mouth
- D) Name the specific feeling to yourself and check whether it belongs to this report

**Q2.** When an earlier, unrelated problem has left you frustrated and a coaching conversation is next on your calendar, you:
- A) Do not separate the two—the frustration carries straight into the conversation
- B) Notice the carryover sometimes, usually after the report reacts to your tone
- C) Actively check whether your mood belongs to this conversation before starting
- D) Treat any carryover as an automatic cue to name it before you speak

**Q3.** When you cannot tell whether your reaction is about the report's performance or something else in your day, you:
- A) Assume it is about the report and proceed
- B) Feel unsure but talk anyway, without naming the uncertainty
- C) Name the uncertainty to yourself and slow down before continuing
- D) Follow a standing rule: an unclear target always gets named before it drives the conversation

**Q4.** Describing what you feel before a hard coaching conversation, you tend to:
- A) Use one word for everything—"upset," "annoyed," "fine"
- B) Recognize a few basic feelings but not the specific one in the moment
- C) Usually land on a specific, accurate word for what you are feeling
- D) Distinguish closely related states (frustrated vs. disappointed vs. anxious) and know why it matters

**Q5.** When a coaching conversation will be documented or affects the report's rating or standing, you:
- A) Treat it the same as any other conversation
- B) Feel more pressure but do not change your preparation
- C) Take extra care to check your own state before starting, given what is riding on it
- D) Treat "record attached" as a named trigger that always gets a state check first

**Q6.** After a coaching conversation that went poorly, you usually discover:
- A) You rarely examine what drove your side of it
- B) You sometimes realize afterward that your tone did not match the issue
- C) You can usually name, after the fact, which feeling leaked into your opening line
- D) You catch the mismatch before it happens, because you checked your state going in

**Q7.** Your direct reports would say your coaching conversations:
- A) Often feel like they are about your mood, not their performance
- B) Sometimes feel that way, depending on the day
- C) Generally feel like they are about their actual performance
- D) Consistently feel grounded in specifics, regardless of what is happening with you that day

**Q8.** Your habit of checking your state before a coaching conversation is:
- A) Not something you do
- B) Something you do occasionally, without a consistent method
- C) A routine step you take before conversations you know are high-stakes
- D) A named practice you use before every coaching conversation, high-stakes or not

--- SECTION 2: SCORING ---

Score A=1, B=2, C=3, D=4. Total range 8–32.

--- SECTION 3: PROFILES ---

1. **Raw Reactor (8–14):** Your state usually walks into the conversation with you, unnamed and unchecked. Reports often absorb feelings that belong to something else in your day. Pick one recurring coaching moment and add a five-second silent label before you speak.
2. **Occasional Checker (15–20):** You catch the mismatch sometimes, usually after your tone has already landed. Build one standing rule: name your state before any conversation with a record attached or a carryover from earlier.
3. **Deliberate Namer (21–26):** You already distinguish a specific feeling from a vague one and check it before most hard conversations. Strengthen it by naming your criteria out loud so reports learn the feedback is about them, not your mood.
4. **State Steward (27–32):** You run a consistent, named check before coaching conversations and keep your reports' feedback usable. Teach one peer your method so the practice survives when you are stretched thin.
