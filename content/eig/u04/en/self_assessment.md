# Self-Assessment — Do You Regulate the Surge or Ride It Out?

Answer for how you **actually** lead this month (A=1 … D=4). Be honest—this is diagnostic, not a performance review.

--- SECTION 1: QUESTIONS ---

**Q1.** When a system or equipment failure floods the floor with alarms and pressure, you usually:
- A) React to whatever hits you first, no pause
- B) Feel the spike and push through it anyway
- C) Notice the spike and slow your breathing before giving the next instruction
- D) Run a set breathing count (in-and-out) before you speak, every time

**Q2.** When a peer, customer, or direct report escalates at you in the moment, you:
- A) Match their energy and respond in kind
- B) Feel the sting but let the first reaction shape your reply anyway
- C) Notice the sting and reframe the moment before responding
- D) Have a standing one-sentence reframe you use to reset before any escalated response

**Q3.** When your own thoughts start spiraling faster than the situation warrants, you:
- A) Let the spiral run and act from wherever it lands
- B) Notice the spiral but usually can't slow it down in time
- C) Deliberately name something physical around you to interrupt it
- D) Use a specific grounding cue (a surface, a sound, a count) as a standing move

**Q4.** Under pressure to respond immediately, your regulation move is:
- A) There isn't one—the first reaction is the response
- B) Inconsistent—sometimes you catch it, sometimes you don't
- C) A quick breath or reframe on the moments that clearly need it
- D) A practiced move you run automatically before any high-stakes response

**Q5.** When several stressors hit at once (an outage plus an escalation plus a deadline), you:
- A) Respond to the loudest one first, unregulated
- B) Feel overwhelmed and react to all of them at similar intensity
- C) Ground first, then decide which stressor actually needs your voice next
- D) Triage using a set method, regulating before addressing each one in turn

**Q6.** Your team can tell when you have used a regulation move before responding:
- A) No—there is no visible difference in how you respond
- B) Rarely—it shows up by accident, not by design
- C) Sometimes—they can see you pause or breathe before a hard call
- D) Yes—there is a visible, team-known cue for when you are regulating

**Q7.** After you use a breathing, grounding, or reframe move, you:
- A) Don't think about whether it worked
- B) Notice if it worked but don't track it
- C) Mentally note whether the move helped, to sharpen it next time
- D) Log which move you used and whether it changed the outcome

**Q8.** Your team's floor culture around regulating a surge in the moment is:
- A) Regulating is seen as hesitation or softness
- B) It happens informally, with no shared method
- C) Taking a breath or a beat before a hard response is expected
- D) The team has a visible, protected ritual (breath, ground, or reframe) that holds even under deadline pressure

--- SECTION 2: SCORING ---

Score A=1, B=2, C=3, D=4. Total range 8–32.

--- SECTION 3: PROFILES ---

1. **Reactive Responder (8–14):** You act on the surge as it lands, including in moments your team or customers will remember for the wrong reasons. Useful when speed genuinely matters; costly when the stakes are high. Pick one recurring trigger and add a two-breath count before you speak.
2. **Occasional Regulator (15–20):** You notice the spike sometimes, usually after your tone has already landed on someone. Build one standing move—breath, ground, or reframe—and run it before you speak, not after.
3. **Deliberate Regulator (21–26):** You already reach for a regulation move on moments that clearly need it. Strengthen it by naming your move out loud so your team learns to expect the beat, not read it as hesitation.
4. **Regulation Steward (27–32):** You run a visible, protected regulation ritual and hold it under pressure. Teach one direct report your specific moves so the ritual survives when you are not on shift.
