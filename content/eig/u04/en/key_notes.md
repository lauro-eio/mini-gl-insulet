# Key Notes — Three Moves for the Surge That's Already Running

**Unit goal:** Give frontline leaders three fast, evidence-based regulation moves—breath, ground, reframe—so a surge that is already running gets steered in the next ten seconds, not just named.

## Core ideas

- **Regulation works best early:** intervening in the first seconds of a surge, before it has shaped your face, voice, or words, costs far less than undoing a reaction the team already saw (Gross, 1998).
- **Reframing changes the body's response, not just the story:** reappraisal reliably lowers physiological arousal compared with pushing through a feeling or suppressing it outwardly (Gross, 2002; Ochsner & Gross, 2005).
- **Slow exhales shift the nervous system fast:** longer exhales than inhales move the body toward calm within the first few breaths, not after extended practice (Jerath et al., 2006; Zaccaro et al., 2018).
- **Grounding redirects attention, not meaning:** naming a concrete physical detail pulls processing capacity off a spiraling thought when a full reframe is not immediately available.
- **Waiting for calm is a choice too:** none of these three moves needs the surge to pass first—delay lets the surge make the call by default.

## Frontline leader checklist

1. Notice the physical signal, then run one move before you speak: exhale longer than you inhale, twice.
2. If the moment has a story attached ("they did this to me"), reframe it in one sentence before you respond.
3. If your mind is spiraling faster than you can reframe, ground first: name one thing you can feel, see, or hear right now.
4. Say the regulated sentence, or send the regulated message—don't wait for the feeling to fully pass.
5. After the shift, note which move you actually used and whether it worked—both sharpen the skill.

## Reflection

Which move—breath, reframe, or ground—do you reach for least on shift, and what would it take to make it automatic?
