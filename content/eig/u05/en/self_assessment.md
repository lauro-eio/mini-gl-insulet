# Self-Assessment — Do You Check Your Read or Just Run With It?

Answer for how you **actually** lead this month (A=1 … D=4). Be honest—this is diagnostic, not a performance review.

--- SECTION 1: QUESTIONS ---

**Q1.** When a teammate gives a short, flat answer during a rushed handoff, you usually:
- A) Take it at face value and move on
- B) Assume you know what's wrong and act on that assumption
- C) Notice the flatness and ask one question before deciding what it means
- D) Reflect back what you noticed and wait for them to confirm or correct it

**Q2.** When you are about to write someone up or reassign them based on their attitude, you:
- A) Act on your read immediately, no check
- B) Mention your concern but move to action fast either way
- C) Check your read with them before deciding what happens next
- D) Treat any disciplinary decision as an automatic cue to reflect-and-check first

**Q3.** When a teammate's tone does not match their words ("I'm fine," said flatly), you:
- A) Go with the words, not the tone
- B) Notice the mismatch but usually let it go
- C) Notice the mismatch and ask about it directly
- D) Follow a standing rule that any mismatch gets a check before you respond

**Q4.** Under deadline pressure, when a quick read of a teammate would save time, you:
- A) Always take the fast read and move
- B) Take the fast read most of the time, check occasionally
- C) Check the read when a real decision is riding on it, skip it when nothing is
- D) Explain to your team, in advance, which moments get a check and why

**Q5.** When you find out later that your read of someone was wrong, you:
- A) Do not think much about it, move on
- B) Feel bad about it but do not change how you read people next time
- C) Note what you missed and what a check would have caught
- D) Log it as evidence for or against trusting that kind of read next time

**Q6.** Your team's floor culture around checking a read before acting on it is:
- A) Checking is seen as slow or as second-guessing
- B) It happens informally, with no shared language
- C) Reflecting a read back before acting is respected and expected
- D) The team has a visible phrase or habit that protects the check even under deadline pressure

**Q7.** When a read of yours turns out to be right, you:
- A) Take it as proof your gut is always right
- B) Feel good about it but do not examine why it worked
- C) Note what made this read accurate, to sharpen it next time
- D) Compare it against named conditions (ambiguous signal, decision riding on it, stakes for someone else)

**Q8.** When a teammate's read of a situation contradicts yours, you:
- A) Defend your read and move on
- B) Consider theirs briefly, then default to yours
- C) Ask what they are seeing that you might be missing
- D) Treat the disagreement itself as a cue to reflect-and-check before either of you acts

--- SECTION 2: SCORING ---

Score A=1, B=2, C=3, D=4. Total range 8–32.

--- SECTION 3: PROFILES ---

1. **Fast Reader (8–14):** You act on almost every read as it lands, including ones a teammate will remember for the wrong reasons. Useful when speed genuinely matters; risky when a decision or someone else's stakes are high. Pick one recurring trigger and add a ten-second reflect-and-check before you act.
2. **Occasional Checker (15–20):** You notice a shaky read sometimes, usually after you have already acted on it. Build one standing rule: check the read when a decision or someone else's stakes are attached, before you act, not after.
3. **Deliberate Listener (21–26):** You already distinguish a confirmed read from an assumed one and check accordingly. Strengthen it by naming your criteria out loud so your team learns to expect the check without being asked.
4. **Read Steward (27–32):** You run a visible, named habit for checking a read and protect it under pressure. Teach one direct report your criteria so the habit survives when you are not on shift.
