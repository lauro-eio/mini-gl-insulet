# Case 2 — Miguel and the Snap on the Dock

## Opening

Miguel coordinates a warehouse dock crew of ten. He has built a reputation for reading people well and rarely feels he needs to ask what is going on—he prides himself on already knowing.

## Context

A new scheduling system just cut Ashley's requested days off for next month without warning. Mid-shift, Ashley snaps at a coworker over a minor mis-scan, sharper than her usual tone. Miguel's first read is immediate: she is upset about the schedule and taking it out on the team. He is ready to pull her aside, remind her the scheduling call was not his, and close the conversation quickly so the shift can keep moving.

A peer supervisor mentions that Ashley has been picking up extra shifts for weeks to cover a sick relative's care costs, on top of her regular load.

## Decision point

Miguel can:

1. Deliver the "the schedule wasn't my call" line now, close the conversation, and get back to the floor.
2. Say what he noticed—the snap, the timing—and ask Ashley one open question about what is actually going on before deciding what she needs.
3. Say nothing about the snap beyond a quick check that the mis-scan is fixed, and note it for a one-on-one later in the week.

## Discussion questions

1. What was Miguel's read actually based on—what he saw in the moment, or what he assumed given the schedule change?
2. How should Miguel weigh the ambiguous signal (the snap) against the possibility that a decision (his response) could land on someone already carrying more than he knows?
3. Design one open question Miguel could ask that invites Ashley to correct his read without putting her on the spot in front of the crew.
