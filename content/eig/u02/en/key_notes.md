# Key Notes — Catching the Hijack Before It Runs the Call

**Unit goal:** Recognize an active hijack in real time — not just the surge before it — and use a brief interrupt to get deliberate judgment back before a floor call locks in the wrong response.

## Core ideas

- **Two roads, one race:** a fast pathway reaches the amygdala directly; a slower pathway routes through the cortex for a fuller read — under pressure, the fast one usually wins (LeDoux, 2000).
- **The fast circuit narrows the room:** once it fires, attention and physiology lock onto the trigger and away from everything else nearby (Phelps & LeDoux, 2005).
- **The slow circuit goes offline, not just quiet:** the same arousal that powers the fast reaction measurably impairs working memory and deliberate judgment (Arnsten, 2009).
- **Three markers of an active hijack:** working memory drops, options collapse to one, and the reaction outlasts the trigger.
- **Naming quiets the circuit in real time:** putting a specific word on the feeling reduces amygdala activity while it is still active, not after (Lieberman et al., 2007).
- **Pressure defaults to habit:** stress shifts behavior toward whatever response is most automatic, right or wrong (Schwabe & Wolf, 2013).

## Frontline leader checklist

1. Check working memory first: can you restate the last real instruction or detail, word for word?
2. If you cannot, say the state out loud: "I'm locked on one move here."
3. Ask yourself one question you cannot fake an answer to before touching anything.
4. Give a peer permission to name your hijack without it reading as a challenge to your authority.
5. Log which marker showed up first today — it sharpens how fast you catch it next time.

## Reflection

Which floor situation most reliably collapses your options down to one move, and what question would force you to check your memory before you act on it?
