# Case 1 — Grant and the Jammed Line He Wanted to Force

## Opening

Grant supervises a packaging line at a mid-sized bottling plant. The feeder on Line 2 has jammed twice already this shift on the same fault code, and a mechanic named Osei has "fixed" it both times. With a customer cutoff eleven minutes away, the feeder jams a third time. Grant feels something in him go flat and narrow — one thought, on a loop: override the interlock himself and force the restart.

## Context

Grant is already walking toward the panel before he registers anything unusual. What stops him, briefly, is smaller than the urge itself: he cannot recall what he told Osei ten minutes earlier during the safety briefing about this exact feeder. He ran that briefing himself. The detail will not come back. Osei is standing six feet away, holding a part he has not yet installed, waiting for Grant to ask him anything at all.

## Decision point

Grant can:

1. Override the interlock himself right now and force the restart, accepting whatever confirmation step he may be skipping in order to hit the cutoff.
2. Say out loud that he is locked on one move, ask himself what the last two checks on the feeder actually were, and only act once he has a real answer — even if it costs the cutoff.
3. Walk away from the panel entirely, hand the decision to the shift lead on the next line over, and deal with the cutoff conversation afterward.

## Discussion questions

1. Which of the three markers — working memory drop, options collapsed to one, or recovery lag — shows up first and most clearly in Grant's moment at the panel?
2. What does the gap in Grant's memory tell him that the tightness in his chest does not?
3. If Grant chooses option 2, what exact question does he ask himself in the ten seconds before touching the panel, and what does Osei's answer change about the decision?
