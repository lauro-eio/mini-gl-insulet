# Self-Assessment — Do You Catch the Hijack or Just Ride It Out?

Answer for how you **actually** lead this month (A=1 … D=4). Be honest — this is diagnostic, not a performance review.

--- SECTION 1: QUESTIONS ---

**Q1.** Mid-crisis on the floor, when you realize you cannot recall the instruction you gave a minute ago, you:
- A) Don't notice — you keep moving on the same track
- B) Notice afterward, once someone asks you to repeat it
- C) Notice in the moment and slow down to check
- D) Treat the memory gap itself as your signal to stop and check before acting

**Q2.** When a jammed process or repeat failure narrows your options down to one forced move, you:
- A) Take that one move immediately, no second look
- B) Take it, then wonder afterward if there was a better option
- C) Notice the narrowing and deliberately name at least one other option
- D) Treat option-narrowing as a named marker and pause until a second option exists

**Q3.** When your reaction to a floor incident is still running minutes after the trigger has passed, you:
- A) Don't track how long it lasts
- B) Notice it's lingering, but let it run its course unmanaged
- C) Notice the lag and consciously work to close it out
- D) Log the lag as a marker and use it to catch the next hijack earlier

**Q4.** When a peer or direct report tells you that you look locked into one move, you:
- A) Dismiss it — you know what you're doing
- B) Hear it but keep going anyway
- C) Pause and actually check what they're seeing
- D) Have a standing agreement that this exact flag gets an immediate pause, no exceptions

**Q5.** Under a hard deadline, when a hijack hits, your default is to:
- A) Push through on instinct — there's no time for anything else
- B) Sometimes catch it, mostly push through anyway
- C) Say the state out loud and ask yourself one grounding question before acting
- D) Run a practiced interrupt — label, working-memory check, then decide — every time

**Q6.** When you notice a peer supervisor is mid-hijack, you:
- A) Say nothing — not your business
- B) Mention it once, then drop it if they wave you off
- C) Name what you're seeing specifically and hold the point briefly
- D) Have a standing, mutual agreement for calling this out without it reading as a challenge

**Q7.** After a moment where you caught your own hijack in time, you:
- A) Don't think about it again
- B) Feel relieved but don't examine what worked
- C) Note which marker tipped you off, to catch it faster next time
- D) Log it and share the specific marker with your team so they learn to spot it too

**Q8.** Your team's floor culture around flagging someone else's hijack in progress is:
- A) Flagging it is seen as insubordination or overstepping
- B) It happens occasionally, inconsistently, with no shared language
- C) Flagging a hijack in a peer is expected and generally welcomed
- D) The team has a specific, practiced phrase or cue for it that survives even under deadline pressure

--- SECTION 2: SCORING ---

Score A=1, B=2, C=3, D=4. Total range 8–32.

--- SECTION 3: PROFILES ---

1. **Hijack Rider (8–14):** You tend to run the full hijack to its end before anything interrupts it, including moves you will need to walk back later. Useful when the first instinct happens to be right; costly when it isn't. Pick one recurring trigger and add a working-memory check — restate the last instruction — before your next move.
2. **Occasional Interrupter (15–20):** You sometimes catch the markers, usually after a peer flags them or the moment has partly passed. Build one standing rule: the moment you cannot restate the last instruction, stop and check before doing anything else.
3. **Reliable Interrupter (21–26):** You already notice working-memory drops and narrowing options and generally interrupt them. Strengthen it by naming your markers out loud so peers learn to flag you the same way you flag yourself.
4. **Interrupt Steward (27–32):** You run a practiced, visible interrupt and extend it to your team, catching hijacks in peers as reliably as your own. Teach one direct report your specific markers so the practice holds when you are off-shift.
