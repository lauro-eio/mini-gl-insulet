# The Low Road and the High Road: What the Evidence Says About Interrupting a Hijack

A supervisor mid-radio-call stares at one detail on a jammed line and misses three others happening around it. Thirty seconds later, they cannot recall the instruction they gave at the start of the call. Neither failure is a lapse in professionalism. Both are the signature of a specific, well-mapped brain state, and the evidence points to a specific way out of it.

## Two roads to a response

LeDoux's mapping of threat circuits describes a fast pathway that reaches the amygdala directly from the senses, and a slower pathway that routes the same input through the cortex for a fuller read of the situation before it drives a response (LeDoux, 2000). Under real pressure, the fast pathway wins the race almost every time. That is not malfunction — it is exactly what the circuit was built to do when speed mattered more than accuracy.

## Why the fast pathway can lock in

Phelps and LeDoux's review of amygdala function shows that once this circuit fires, it recruits attention and physiology toward whatever triggered it and away from everything else in the room (Phelps & LeDoux, 2005). This is why a supervisor can fixate on the one detail that provoked them and genuinely not register the other three things a calmer version of themselves would have caught in the same ten seconds.

## The slower pathway does not just lose the race — it goes offline

Arnsten's work on stress and the prefrontal cortex found that the same arousal fueling the fast circuit actively impairs the brain region responsible for working memory and deliberate judgment (Arnsten, 2009). A leader mid-hijack is not simply distracted; they are running with measurably reduced capacity to hold the last instruction in mind. This explains a pattern many supervisors recognize on shift: forgetting the second half of a handoff they gave less than a minute earlier.

## Three markers an active hijack shows on the floor

The evidence points to three observable signs, useful because they can be checked in the moment rather than diagnosed after the fact:

1. **Working memory drops** — the person cannot restate the last step or instruction they just gave or received.
2. **Options collapse to one** — the same reflexive move surfaces regardless of the specifics, with no real weighing of alternatives.
3. **Recovery lags** — the reaction visibly outlasts the trigger by minutes, not seconds.

One marker may be noise. Two or more, especially working memory loss, indicates the fast circuit is currently running the call.

## Naming interrupts the circuit itself, not just the mood

Lieberman and colleagues found that putting a specific word to what a person is feeling measurably reduces amygdala activity while the feeling is still active — not afterward, during (Lieberman et al., 2007). A short label is not a soft coping habit layered on top of the problem. It is a documented way to quiet the circuit that is currently steering the call.

## Under pressure, behavior defaults to habit

Schwabe and Wolf's research on stress and memory systems found that acute stress shifts behavior away from deliberate, goal-directed choices and toward whichever response is most habitual for that person — useful when the habit happens to be the right one, costly when it is not (Schwabe & Wolf, 2013). This is one reason the same experienced supervisor can make a sound call on a routine day and a poor one under real pressure: pressure does not erase skill, it routes the decision through a different system.

## What this means for a floor leader

Noticing a surge, the skill from the prior unit, buys a few seconds before a reaction starts. Once a hijack is already running — working memory gone, options collapsed, feeling outlasting the trigger — a leader needs an active interrupt, not a pause: a specific label, then a working-memory question deliberate enough to force the slower circuit back online before the next word leaves their mouth.
