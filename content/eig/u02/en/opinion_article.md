# The Restart I Almost Forced During the Hijack

The line had jammed for the third time that shift, same feeder, same fault code. I had a customer cutoff in eleven minutes and a mechanic who had already told me twice that it was fixed. When it jammed a third time, something in me just went flat and narrow. I remember walking toward the panel with one thought only: override the interlock myself and force the restart. Not a plan. Not a weighed decision. One move, repeating in my head like it was the only option that existed.

I had my hand on the panel before I noticed anything was wrong. What stopped me was small and almost embarrassing: I could not remember what I had told the mechanic ten minutes earlier during the safety briefing about that exact feeder. I had run it that morning. I could not get it back. That gap is what made me pause — not good judgment, just the unsettling feeling of reaching for a memory that would not come.

I said it out loud, to nobody in particular: "I'm locked on one move here." Then I made myself answer a real question before touching anything else: what were the last two checks on that feeder, and had either one actually been confirmed. I did not know. That alone should have ended the conversation before it started.

It turned out the mechanic's second fix had not been signed off — he was waiting on a part, and I had walked past that exact detail twice without registering it. If I had forced the restart, I would have run an unconfirmed interlock override on a line that had already jammed twice, to save maybe four minutes, with a mechanic standing right there who could have told me in ten seconds if I had asked instead of reached.

I do not think the flat, narrow feeling is going away, and I am not sure I would want it to — it shows up because the stakes are real. What I am trying to change is what happens in the ten seconds after it lands. Naming it out loud costs almost nothing. Asking myself a question I actually have to answer, instead of a question I can wave off, costs a few more seconds and forces the slower part of my thinking back into the room.

I told the mechanic afterward, and I told my own crew the same week: if you notice you cannot recall the last real detail of a situation you are about to act on, that is the signal, not the tightness in your chest. Say it. Ask yourself the question you cannot fake an answer to. Then decide.
